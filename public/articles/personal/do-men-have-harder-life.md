# Do Men Have a Harder Life Than Women?

## Part 1

The question sounds simple:

**Do men have a harder life than women?**

My answer is complicated.

Men can have extremely difficult lives. Women can have extremely difficult lives too. But I don't think their difficulties can be measured on the same scale because many of the pressures they face are fundamentally different.

Since I have never lived outside India and my understanding of other societies is limited, I am restricting this discussion to Indian society and to the experiences I have observed around me.

I also want to look at this generation by generation because the environment in which someone grows up can completely change the nature of their problems.

---

# The Life of a Man

In many Indian families, a boy's future is discussed almost as soon as he is born.

What will he become?

What will he study?

How successful will he be?

How much will he earn?

Who will depend on him?

A boy may be loved deeply during childhood, but as he grows older, expectations begin to replace some of that emotional protection.

Around the age of six or seven, many boys are gradually encouraged to become more independent. The child who once slept beside his mother and freely expressed his emotions may suddenly be expected to behave "like a boy."

Don't cry.

Be strong.

Handle it yourself.

Study properly.

Become successful.

Eventually, these expectations become part of his identity.

But the experience differs considerably across generations.

## Generation 1: Born Before 2004

For many boys from this generation, childhood was relatively simple.

They played with friends, spent time with their families, went to school, fought with friends, made up again, and continued growing up.

Academic pressure existed from an early age, but the major transition usually came around secondary school.

Until Class 10, many subjects remained manageable. Then Class 11 arrived and suddenly everything became more difficult.

At the same time, the question of the future became unavoidable.

NEET.

JEE.

CA.

UPSC.

Graduation.

Scholarships.

Employment.

Family responsibilities.

For families with limited financial resources, the pressure could become even heavier because a boy's education might be seen not only as his personal future but as an investment in the entire family.

Then adolescence added another layer.

Hormonal changes, attraction, love, intimacy, and sexuality began entering their lives without most of them having enough emotional or sexual education to understand what they were experiencing.

Popular culture and conversations with friends often became the primary sources of information.

Many boys experienced their first serious attraction or relationship during this period.

And many also experienced their first heartbreak.

For someone experiencing intense emotional attachment for the first time, losing that relationship can feel devastating.

The combination of heartbreak, academic pressure, uncertainty about the future, family expectations, and loneliness can become overwhelming.

Some boys develop unhealthy coping mechanisms.

Some turn toward smoking, alcohol, or other harmful habits.

Some withdraw emotionally.

Some develop a defensive worldview such as:

**"Girls cannot be trusted."**

After another painful relationship, that belief can become even stronger.

Eventually, an individual experience becomes a general rule in their mind.

One person betrayed me.

Therefore, everyone will betray me.

That is not logically sound, but emotional pain rarely cares about logic.

For some men, however, the pressure becomes much more serious. Academic failure, unemployment, financial responsibility, family expectations, isolation, and mental-health struggles can combine into a dangerous situation.

Some survive that period and become stronger.

Some don't.

This is one of the reasons I think male suffering should not be dismissed simply because men are often perceived as having more freedom.

Freedom does not automatically mean emotional safety.

---

# Generation 2: Born Around 2006–2010

The environment changed significantly for this generation.

The biggest difference was technology.

Children who previously had to depend on books, teachers, television, or older people for information suddenly had the entire internet in their hands.

That created enormous advantages.

A student could learn mathematics from YouTube.

Find programming tutorials.

Read about science.

Access educational material from universities.

Learn skills that their parents had never even heard of.

But the same technology created a completely different problem.

Children were exposed to adult information much earlier than previous generations.

And because parental digital literacy was often limited, many parents did not know how easily children could encounter inappropriate content online.

Curiosity that previously developed gradually could now be triggered instantly.

A child could encounter sexual content accidentally through advertisements, social media, websites, or other online recommendations.

Once curiosity appeared, the internet could continuously provide more material.

This is where pornography became particularly influential.

The problem is not simply that teenagers discovered sexual content.

The deeper problem is that pornography can become a substitute for actual sexual education.

A teenager may learn what sex supposedly looks like without learning enough about consent, communication, contraception, emotional intimacy, boundaries, or the difference between fantasy and reality.

That can create unrealistic expectations about relationships and sexuality.

Social media adds another layer.

Instagram, YouTube, short-video platforms, and similar services constantly expose young people to carefully selected images of other people's lives.

For some boys, repeated exposure to sexualized content can gradually change how they perceive women.

A person can stop seeing a woman primarily as another human being and begin unconsciously viewing her through a sexual lens.

That is a serious problem.

And when this develops alongside academic pressure, poor sleep, excessive screen time, social isolation, and weak emotional education, the result can be a very unstable adolescence.

---

# The College Years

College creates another transition.

A boy who spent years being told to study suddenly receives an enormous amount of freedom.

New friends.

New environment.

No one constantly monitoring him.

For some students, this becomes an opportunity to grow.

For others, it becomes an opportunity to avoid responsibility.

Some discover productive habits.

Others discover how little effort they can put into studying while still passing.

The danger is that temporary success can create false confidence.

A student studies only the night before an examination, passes, and concludes:

**"I am smarter than everyone thinks."**

That pattern can continue for years.

Then final year arrives.

Suddenly there is a degree to complete, a career to build, a job to find, and a future to construct.

The consequences of earlier decisions become impossible to ignore.

Some recover.

Some don't.

Eventually, many men enter employment, marriage, and family life carrying the same unresolved beliefs they developed during adolescence.

Some still believe that women are untrustworthy.

Some still consume pornography as though it represents real intimacy.

Some enter marriage without understanding emotional responsibility.

And when those beliefs enter a marriage, both partners can suffer.

So the difficulties men face are not limited to earning money.

They can include emotional suppression, unrealistic expectations, academic pressure, career uncertainty, financial responsibility, loneliness, relationship failures, and the expectation that they should remain strong regardless of what is happening internally.

---

# The Life of a Woman

The female experience in India can be difficult for very different reasons.

In many families, a girl is still raised with the idea that she will eventually leave her parents' home and become part of another family.

That idea can influence almost everything.

Her freedom.

Her education.

Her friendships.

Her clothing.

Her mobility.

Her career.

Her relationships.

Her marriage.

A young girl may have almost the same freedom as a boy during early childhood.

But as she grows older, restrictions can gradually increase.

She may be told not to go outside alone.

She may be discouraged from spending time with boys.

She may be expected to learn household work.

She may be taught to be cautious around strangers because of the risk of harassment or sexual violence.

Some of these restrictions come from genuinely justified concerns about safety.

But the problem is that society often responds to unsafe environments by restricting women's freedom instead of fixing the environment itself.

The result is that girls can grow up learning that the world is dangerous and that their behaviour must constantly be adjusted around that danger.

---

# Adolescence and Menstruation

Another major difference is menstruation.

A girl eventually has to deal with a biological process that can involve pain, fatigue, mood changes, discomfort, and social embarrassment.

The physical experience itself is not necessarily the hardest part.

What can make it worse is the environment around it.

Imagine being in pain but still being expected to perform household responsibilities normally.

Imagine having an important examination while dealing with severe cramps.

Imagine feeling physically exhausted but being told that the work still has to be completed.

The problem becomes larger when family members dismiss the pain or treat menstruation as something inconvenient or shameful.

I once conducted an informal survey by asking girls about their experiences during menstruation, including what made them uncomfortable, what they found most difficult, and how they balanced studying and household responsibilities.

The responses made one thing particularly clear to me:

Sometimes the most painful part isn't the physical discomfort.

It is feeling ignored when you need support.

That distinction matters.

A person can tolerate physical discomfort much more easily when they feel understood.

---

# Education and Financial Independence

After school, another major difference appears.

Some girls receive strong support for higher education.

Others don't.

In families with conservative attitudes, a daughter's education may still be treated as less important than a son's.

Financial limitations can make this even worse.

Some girls therefore have to work earlier than they expected.

Others combine education with employment.

Some have to contribute to household responsibilities simultaneously.

So a young woman may eventually find herself balancing:

**Education + Work + Household Responsibilities + Family Expectations**

That is an enormous workload for anyone.

And unlike academic or professional responsibilities, household labour is often unpaid and invisible.

There is no salary for cooking dinner.

No promotion for raising children.

No annual bonus for maintaining a household.

Yet these activities can consume a huge amount of time.

---

# Generation 2 Women

Technology has also changed the lives of younger women.

Many girls now receive smartphones during secondary school.

That provides greater access to education, communication, entertainment, and social interaction.

But it also introduces new pressures.

Social media can become a substitute for physical freedom.

A girl who is restricted from going outside may spend more time online.

The internet becomes her social environment.

That can be empowering, but it can also create another set of problems: excessive screen time, comparison, online validation, cyberbullying, unhealthy relationships, and constant exposure to idealized lifestyles.

Relationships can become particularly complicated.

A boy may not understand why his girlfriend is emotionally exhausted.

A girl may not feel comfortable explaining what she is experiencing.

The boy may interpret distance as rejection.

She may interpret his suspicion as a lack of trust.

Both begin creating theories about each other.

Communication decreases.

Suspicion increases.

Eventually, the relationship collapses.

The problem isn't necessarily that either person is evil.

Sometimes they simply don't have the emotional education required to understand each other.

---

# Marriage Changes the Equation Again

Marriage can create another dramatic transition for women.

A woman may have to leave the environment in which she grew up and enter a completely new household.

New people.

New expectations.

New routines.

New responsibilities.

And sometimes, she barely knows the person she has married.

This is particularly concerning in situations where the marriage was heavily controlled by families and the woman's preferences were not given enough importance.

I have sometimes wondered whether the tears associated with a woman's wedding are always primarily about leaving her parents.

I don't know.

That is something I cannot claim with certainty.

Perhaps sometimes the deeper fear is the uncertainty of what comes next.

She may be leaving the only environment she knows and entering a life whose conditions she cannot fully predict.

And unlike moving to another city for education or employment, marriage can involve a much deeper and more permanent change in personal and family relationships.

---

# After Marriage

The workload can increase dramatically after marriage, particularly when the woman is expected to manage both employment and the household.

Imagine a typical day:

Wake up early.

Prepare breakfast.

Prepare children's meals.

Prepare the husband's meals.

Get the children ready.

Clean the kitchen.

Clean the house.

Wash clothes.

Go to work, if employed.

Return home.

Take care of the children.

Help with homework.

Prepare evening meals.

Clean again.

Go to bed.

Repeat.

This isn't necessarily the life of every Indian woman.

Families differ enormously.

But where this pattern exists, the problem is obvious:

**There may be no real day off.**

A man with a conventional job may have Saturday or Sunday away from professional work.

A woman carrying most household responsibilities may not have an equivalent weekend.

The house still needs cleaning.

Children still need attention.

Food still needs to be prepared.

Laundry still exists.

The responsibilities don't disappear because it is Sunday.

And if the woman takes a week away from those responsibilities, the household may immediately feel the impact.

That is the invisible nature of domestic labour.

People notice it most clearly when it stops.

---

# Motherhood Adds Another Layer

Pregnancy introduces another enormous physical and emotional responsibility.

The pregnancy itself can be difficult.

Then comes childbirth.

Then recovery.

Then childcare.

A newborn often depends heavily on the mother, particularly in the early stages.

Sleep becomes fragmented.

Personal time decreases.

Household responsibilities may continue.

Professional responsibilities may continue.

And society may still expect the mother to perform everything perfectly.

Meanwhile, the husband may continue living a comparatively conventional working life.

This is where I think comparisons between male and female difficulty become especially misleading.

A man's responsibilities can be enormous.

But a woman's responsibilities can involve physical, emotional, domestic, professional, and reproductive burdens simultaneously.

They are different kinds of pressure.

---

# So, Who Has the Harder Life?

This is where my answer changed while writing this article.

Initially, I believed the answer was obvious:

**Men have harder lives.**

And I still believe that male life can become extremely difficult.

Men can face enormous expectations around earning, career success, family responsibility, emotional control, and financial stability.

But after looking more carefully at the female experience, I don't think it is intellectually honest to simply declare that men have it harder.

The comparison itself is flawed.

A man's difficulties and a woman's difficulties often exist in completely different dimensions.

A man may be expected to provide.

A woman may be expected to provide while also maintaining the household.

A man may experience loneliness and emotional suppression.

A woman may experience restricted freedom and constant safety concerns.

A man may face career pressure.

A woman may face career pressure while simultaneously being expected to manage domestic responsibilities.

A man may be expected to remain emotionally strong.

A woman may be expected to remain emotionally strong while also carrying physical and reproductive burdens that men simply do not experience.

Neither of these realities cancels the other.

And that is why saying **"men have it harder"** or **"women have it harder"** doesn't tell us very much.

It turns two complicated human experiences into a competition.

Life isn't a competition where one gender wins the suffering trophy.

---

# My Conclusion

If I look only at the responsibilities placed on men, I can understand why someone would say that men's lives are difficult.

If I look at the restrictions, safety concerns, reproductive burden, domestic expectations, and social limitations placed on women, I can understand why someone would say that women's lives are even more difficult.

Personally, after examining both sides, I would not rank them.

I would describe them as **different systems of pressure**.

Men are often pushed toward becoming providers.

Women are often pushed toward becoming caregivers.

Men may receive greater freedom but greater expectations of financial responsibility.

Women may receive less freedom while carrying greater expectations around care, family, and domestic responsibility.

And both can suffer when society reduces them to those roles.

That is why the better question may not be:

**"Who has the harder life?"**

It may be:

**"Why do we keep designing people's lives around expectations they never chose?"**

A boy shouldn't have to become emotionally numb simply because he is male.

A girl shouldn't have to surrender her freedom simply because she is female.

A man shouldn't be valued only for what he earns.

A woman shouldn't be valued only for what she does for her family.

Both deserve the ability to choose what their lives mean.

So, yes, men's lives can be extremely difficult.

Women's lives can be extremely difficult too.

But comparing them directly is like comparing two mountains simply because both require climbing.

The better question is not which mountain is harder.

It is whether we can make either mountain easier to climb.
