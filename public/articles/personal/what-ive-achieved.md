# What I've Achieved in My Life and How

Until now, I have never really known how to list my achievements properly.

If someone asks me what I have achieved in life, I don't immediately think about awards, ranks, certificates, or material accomplishments. The things I consider my biggest achievements are much more personal:

**Emotional control, clarity about relationships, mutual understanding, self-awareness, and, to a smaller extent, discipline.**

Alongside these, I have also developed several frameworks and learning systems, including **CDMA (Cognitive Dual Multi-Adaptation)**, **PSI (Psychological Substitution with Illusion)**, and a **gamified self-regulated learning system** based on conditional tasks.

The strange part is that I never consciously planned most of these things.

Almost everything started as an accident, curiosity, frustration, overthinking, or even something I was doing purely for fun.

Only later did I realize that some of those experiments had turned into something meaningful.

## Emotional Control

The first major change happened on **11 November 2022**.

It was one of the worst days of my life. Someone I cared about hurt me deeply, and I spent that day overwhelmed with sadness. I cried more than I ever had before.

But something changed after that day.

The person I was on 10 November and the person I became after 11 November were noticeably different.

I don't remember consciously deciding to become emotionally stronger. It simply happened.

Over time, I became better at controlling my tears, sadness, excitement, and, to some extent, anger.

Looking back now, I think I understand what may have happened.

Whenever I reached an extreme emotional or psychological limit, something inside me seemed to adapt. My existing emotional boundaries were pushed, and eventually my ability to handle those emotions became stronger.

This idea later became connected to my CDMA framework.

What initially looked like emotional damage eventually became one of the experiences that changed how I regulate myself.

## Clarity and Mutual Understanding

Another major development came from something that originally had nothing to do with self-improvement.

I created an imaginary world.

At first, I created it simply because I needed somewhere to escape from reality. But I didn't want a fantasy world where everything was perfect.

I wanted it to behave like reality.

People had emotions.

People had different personalities.

People made mistakes.

There were conflicts.

There were consequences.

There were rewards.

There were good people and difficult people.

There were misunderstandings.

There were motivations behind people's actions.

So I started constructing situations and trying to understand how different people would behave in them.

Without realizing it, I was constantly examining human behaviour from different perspectives.

Eventually, that started affecting how I understood real relationships.

I became better at separating what someone said from what they might actually mean.

I became more interested in understanding motivations rather than simply judging actions.

I also developed a stronger sense of what creates mutual understanding between two people.

Interestingly, my ability to understand something is sometimes much better than my ability to explain it in words.

I may understand an idea internally in considerable depth but struggle to communicate the same depth in a perfectly structured way.

That is something I am still improving.

## Self-Awareness

Self-awareness developed in a similar way.

While building imaginary situations, I eventually stopped looking only at other people.

I started looking at myself.

I would imagine myself from different perspectives and ask:

Why did I behave like that?

What was actually motivating me?

Was my decision logical or emotional?

Was an action good, bad, or both depending on the situation?

What could happen if I continued a particular habit?

What would the positive and negative consequences be?

What weaknesses do I have?

What patterns keep repeating in my behaviour?

This became a continuous process of observing myself almost like I was observing another person.

The interesting thing is that I didn't even realize I had developed strong self-awareness.

For almost two years, I simply considered it normal thinking.

It was only later, after discussing these patterns with ChatGPT, that I started recognizing that what I was doing was actually a form of self-awareness.

Discipline is a different story.

I still wouldn't describe myself as highly disciplined.

I have some discipline, but even now I don't think I fully understand what discipline means in its complete sense.

Whatever amount I have developed probably came from frustration, anger, pressure, and the need to keep moving when motivation wasn't available.

## PSI Framework: Psychological Substitution with Illusion

The first framework I deliberately started using was PSI, which stands for **Psychological Substitution with Illusion**.

I originally created it for examinations.

The basic idea was to influence how my preparation and presentation were perceived by teachers.

For example, I would prepare notes before a semester officially became serious and sometimes show them to my teachers for verification.

That created a particular impression.

The teacher knew that I had prepared in advance.

They recognized my name.

They became familiar with me as a student who appeared academically engaged.

That perception could later influence how they interpreted my work.

I also noticed that I could often predict answers even when I hadn't formally studied the topic in depth. Instead of presenting myself as someone who already knew everything, I sometimes deliberately behaved as though I was still learning.

The purpose was not simply to display knowledge but to maintain a particular perception.

The second part of PSI was examination presentation.

If I didn't know an answer completely, I would reconstruct it using whatever related knowledge I had.

I might include:

- Relevant theory
- Key points
- Working or methodology
- Diagrams
- Explanation of the diagram
- Code where applicable
- Logical connections between concepts
- A final explanation connecting the answer together

The result was a detailed answer that looked substantially more complete than the amount of knowledge I initially possessed.

The psychological component was important because presentation influences perception.

A well-structured answer with headings, diagrams, explanations, and logical progression can communicate competence before every individual sentence is even examined closely.

I noticed this effect repeatedly in my examinations.

There were situations where my actual answer was not perfectly aligned with the expected answer, yet the overall structure and reasoning still produced surprisingly good results.

One example was a Mobile Application Development examination where I answered a question using concepts related to navigation and routing. Despite the mismatch, I received full marks.

That experience made me realize that examination performance is not always determined solely by how much information you remember.

It can also depend on how effectively you reconstruct, communicate, and present what you know.

That became the basis of PSI.

## CDMA: Cognitive Dual Multi-Adaptation

CDMA was even more accidental.

It happened during my end-semester examinations.

I had Full Stack Development on one day and Mathematics the next day.

The problem was that I was badly prepared for both.

My knowledge of HTML was basic, while CSS and JavaScript were even weaker. Mathematics was another problem because I had already failed it once.

I was stuck between two choices.

If I focused on Full Stack, Mathematics would suffer.

If I focused on Mathematics, Full Stack would suffer.

The stress kept increasing.

Eventually, I opened my Full Stack notes.

I didn't properly study them.

I spent roughly ten minutes scrolling through five units.

Then I felt mentally exhausted and went to sleep.

The next day, I went to college without revising again.

When I received the examination paper, something strange happened.

As soon as I started reading the questions, the material I had looked at for those few minutes suddenly felt familiar.

The code I had seen the previous night appeared surprisingly clear in my memory.

I didn't remember consciously memorizing it.

I simply started writing.

Whenever I didn't remember something exactly, I reconstructed it through logic and general understanding.

I finished the paper.

Later, I became curious about what had happened.

I began researching the phenomenon and eventually developed my own framework around it.

I call it **Cognitive Dual Multi-Adaptation**.

The basic idea, as I currently understand it, is that under extreme cognitive pressure, my brain sometimes appears to shift into a highly adaptive state where a short period of exposure can produce unusually strong temporary performance.

The result can be extremely useful.

But there is a serious cost.

After reaching that state, I experience significant mental fatigue.

The recovery period can last several days.

So CDMA is not something I consider a perfect learning method.

It is more like an emergency cognitive adaptation mechanism that I happened to notice in myself.

The interesting part is that I didn't invent it because I wanted to create a framework.

The framework came afterward.

First came the experience.

Then came the question:

**"What just happened?"**

Then came the observation.

Then the framework.

## A Gamified Self-Regulated Learning System

The last major development began even more casually.

I was creating study material and randomly adding some funny and interactive elements to it.

At some point, I thought:

What if notes themselves could behave like an interactive system?

So I started adding conditional tasks.

If a condition was true, the learner had to perform a task.

If it was false, the task could be skipped.

Then I added special tasks.

Then I introduced elements inspired by games.

Eventually, I created a rule-based system with different commandments and conditions.

What started as me simply making study material less boring eventually became something much more interesting.

The idea was no longer just:

**Read → Memorize → Take Exam**

Instead, it became:

**Learn → Interact → Make Decisions → Complete Conditions → Receive Feedback → Continue Learning**

That completely changed how I thought about educational material.

I started seeing notes not as static documents but as systems that could respond to the learner.

This is now something I want to develop further and eventually turn into a research paper.

If that happens, this small experiment could become the beginning of my research journey.

## The Pattern Behind All of It

When I look at everything together, there is a strange pattern.

I didn't sit down one day and decide:

"I am going to develop emotional control."

I didn't decide:

"I am going to develop high-level self-awareness."

I didn't plan:

"I am going to invent CDMA."

I didn't begin studying psychology with the goal of creating PSI.

And I certainly didn't start designing interactive learning systems because I thought I was going to develop a research direction.

Most of these things started with something much smaller.

Pain.

Frustration.

Curiosity.

Overthinking.

Experimentation.

Or sometimes just doing something stupid for fun.

The valuable part came later, when I started asking questions about what had happened.

Why did I react differently?

Why did this technique work?

Why did my brain behave differently under stress?

Why did people perceive my work differently?

Why did this learning system feel more engaging?

Those questions transformed random experiences into structured ideas.

## What I Actually Consider My Achievement

So, if I had to summarize what I have achieved so far, I wouldn't simply list the frameworks.

My bigger achievement is that I learned how to observe myself.

I learned to question my own behaviour.

I learned to examine relationships instead of simply experiencing them.

I developed some control over my emotions.

I became more aware of my strengths and weaknesses.

And I started converting unusual experiences into ideas that could potentially become frameworks, systems, or research.

The frameworks are simply the visible results of that process.

The more important achievement is the process itself.

I don't know how many of these ideas will eventually become academically valid frameworks.

Some may be wrong.

Some may need substantial modification.

Some may turn out to be observations that only apply to me.

And some may become genuinely useful.

I'm still figuring that out.

But there is one thing I have noticed about my life.

Many of the things I consider valuable today began as something I didn't take seriously.

A painful experience became emotional control.

An imaginary world became a way of understanding people.

Overthinking became self-awareness.

Exam experimentation became PSI.

Extreme academic stress became CDMA.

Randomly making study material interactive became a learning system.

And that may be the most interesting thing I have achieved so far:

**I have learned to turn mistakes, accidents, curiosity, and even childish experiments into something worth investigating.**

Maybe that is how my journey as a researcher will begin.

Not with one perfect idea.

Not with one extraordinary discovery.

But with a stupid little experiment that makes me curious enough to ask:

**"What happens if I take this seriously?"**
