# What I Think About a Healthy Relationship and Why

A healthy relationship is one of those topics where my thoughts can easily turn into a long chain of ideas. Instead of defining it with one sentence, I understand it better by looking at different relationships: siblings, parents and children, friends, and couples.

For me, a healthy relationship is built on four main pillars:

1. Mutual Understanding
2. Transparency
3. Loyalty
4. Respect

Other qualities such as love, trust, care, and support are also important, but I see them as things that naturally become stronger when these four pillars are maintained.

## A Healthy Relationship Between Siblings

Siblings fighting with each other is normal. For me, the number of arguments is not what determines whether their relationship is healthy. What matters is how they handle those conflicts.

A healthy relationship between siblings should not be based on hatred or jealousy toward each other's progress. There should be love, trust, moral support, and care.

If one sibling is going through a difficult situation, the other should try to make them feel better instead of making the situation worse. They should be able to talk about their problems and vulnerabilities without constantly worrying that the other person will expose them.

They should also be willing to stand beside each other during difficult situations, both emotionally and practically.

I think this kind of relationship can also teach valuable skills. Growing up with a sibling can create opportunities to develop teamwork, leadership, decision-making, critical thinking, problem-solving, and the ability to understand another person's perspective.

The important thing is not that siblings never fight. It is that the relationship remains stronger than the individual conflicts.

## A Healthy Relationship Between Parents and Children

Imagine that I am a father, Rida is my wife, and we have four children.

In the kind of family I consider healthy, my family would be my highest priority. That does not mean completely abandoning work or personal responsibilities, but it means making sure my wife and children receive enough time, attention, and emotional presence.

From their early childhood, I would want to create an environment where learning is part of everyday life rather than something limited to school.

I would spend time teaching them myself. I would introduce them to puzzles, mathematics, logical reasoning, physical activities, football, running, and other forms of learning. I would keep their screen time reasonably limited and encourage them to explore the world directly.

Sometimes I might even have to learn something myself before teaching it to them. I would be completely willing to do that.

More importantly, I would want to develop their ability to think rather than simply memorize information.

For example, I might ask:

"If you had two choices, which would you prefer: knowing everything that will happen in your future, including everything good and bad that people might do to you, or living normally without knowing what the future holds? Which life would you choose and why?"

Or:

"If you had the power to permanently eliminate three types of crime from the world, which three would you choose and why?"

As they grow older, I would increase the complexity of these questions.

But education would not be the most important part of the relationship.

I would want to remain their father while also being someone they feel safe talking to. They will make mistakes. Sometimes they may break my trust unintentionally because of curiosity, immaturity, or poor decisions.

My response should not automatically be anger, insults, humiliation, or physical punishment.

I want my children to know that making a mistake does not mean losing their father's love.

A healthy parent-child relationship, in my view, should create a balance between guidance and independence. Parents should teach children how to think, make decisions, understand consequences, and eventually become capable of living without constant parental control.

## A Healthy Relationship Between Friends

Imagine I meet a person named Ayesha during my MCA.

Initially, we are strangers. After spending time together, we become friends, and eventually we become comfortable enough to discuss personal problems.

Suppose Ayesha tells me about her vulnerabilities.

Once someone trusts me with something personal, I believe I have a responsibility to protect that information rather than use it against them.

But I would also want to help her understand something important: trust should not mean blindly trusting everyone.

For example, suppose she has a part-time work-from-home job and earns some money independently. I might explain how someone who becomes close to her could potentially identify that source of income and later try to take advantage of her trust.

I would explain these possibilities not to make her suspicious of everyone, but to make her aware of how exploitation can happen.

Understanding someone's vulnerabilities creates responsibility.

If I know exactly what could emotionally hurt a person, financially exploit them, or make them easier to control, I should not use that knowledge against them.

In fact, I should help them understand their own vulnerabilities so they can protect themselves.

I would support her when she needs help, help her with studies, introduce her to new ways of thinking, and encourage her to look at the same situation from different perspectives.

For me, the difference between a healthy and unhealthy friendship can sometimes be as simple as this:

The unhealthy friend discovers your weakness and thinks, "How can I use this?"

The healthy friend discovers your weakness and thinks, "How can I help you protect yourself from someone who might use this?"

That distinction matters enormously to me.

## A Healthy Relationship Between Couples

This is the relationship where I think the four pillars become most important.

Imagine that after completing my M.Tech, I am introduced to a woman named Rida Rahman through an arranged marriage.

I do not know her personally, but her father knows me well and has suggested the marriage.

Before agreeing, I would want to meet Rida privately and have an honest conversation.

I would first ask her whether she is comfortable with me asking some personal questions. Then I would explain that I want transparent answers, even if some answers might be uncomfortable for me.

My first question would be:

"Do you actually want to marry me? Is this marriage happening because you want it, or because your parents are pressuring you?"

I would also ask whether there is already someone else in her life whom she wants to marry but cannot because of family, caste, religious, or other circumstances.

Then I would ask about her expectations from a husband, her understanding of parenting, how she imagines raising children, and how she expects both partners to contribute time, effort, energy, and emotions to the marriage.

I would also give her the opportunity to ask me anything she wants.

Compatibility cannot be one-sided.

If I expect transparency from her, she should receive the same transparency from me.

If we eventually decide to marry, I would want the marriage itself to remain our decision rather than something controlled entirely by social expectations.

## The First Night and Why a Relationship Should Come Before Intimacy

Imagine that after our marriage, we reach our room and I notice that Rida is nervous.

Instead of assuming that marriage automatically means physical intimacy, I would ask her whether she is scared or uncomfortable.

If she says that she is nervous, I would tell her that nothing has to happen that night.

There is no deadline.

For me, marriage does not create automatic entitlement to physical intimacy.

I would want her to first become comfortable with me, our environment, our habits, and our new life together.

I might tell her something like:

"Your life is not controlled by what people have traditionally done. Our decisions about our relationship should belong to both of us."

This does not mean ignoring our families, culture, religion, or society. They can give advice and guidance. But ultimately, the two people living the marriage have to build it themselves.

That is where the four pillars become important.

## 1. Mutual Understanding

Suppose we repeatedly fail to understand each other's efforts and sacrifices.

I might not understand what she is doing for our family, while she might not understand the pressures I experience at work.

Even if there is physical intimacy between us, the relationship can still become unhealthy.

Mutual understanding means trying to understand the person behind the action.

It does not mean agreeing with everything.

It means being willing to ask:

"Why did you do this?"

"What were you feeling?"

"What were you trying to achieve?"

"What can I understand from your perspective?"

That process takes time.

## 2. Transparency

This is why I would replace "honesty" with "transparency."

To me, transparency means creating an environment where both partners can communicate their real thoughts, feelings, discomfort, expectations, and concerns without having to hide important information.

For example, if I ask:

"Do you want to go somewhere with me?"

and she does not want to go, she should feel completely comfortable saying no.

She should not think:

"If I say no, he will feel hurt."

If we have mutual understanding, I should be able to accept that answer.

The same applies to larger issues.

If something is bothering her, I want her to tell me.

If something is bothering me, I should tell her.

Transparency reduces the need for guessing games.

## 3. Loyalty

Loyalty does not mean that either partner can never talk to another person of the opposite sex.

My job might require me to interact with women. I might meet an old female classmate. She might have male colleagues or friends.

That alone does not violate loyalty.

For me, loyalty means maintaining the boundaries of the relationship even when opportunities to cross them exist.

It means that I can interact normally with other people while remaining committed to my partner.

If something happens that could reasonably make my partner uncomfortable, transparency and mutual understanding should allow us to discuss it rather than hide it.

Loyalty is therefore not about controlling who your partner can speak to.

It is about what you choose to do with the trust your partner has placed in you.

If either partner is having an affair, intentionally deceiving the other, or treating the relationship as disposable, then physical intimacy cannot compensate for the damage.

A couple could have an active physical relationship and still have an unhealthy marriage.

## 4. Respect

This is the pillar I consider non-negotiable.

Arguments will happen.

Disagreements will happen.

Sometimes both people will misunderstand each other.

But anger does not justify abuse.

If either partner starts insulting, humiliating, threatening, or physically harming the other, something fundamental has broken.

Even if the couple later apologizes and returns to normal life, abuse can permanently damage the sense of safety within the relationship.

That is why respect cannot be treated as something that exists only when both people are happy.

Respect matters most when both people are angry.

A healthy relationship is not one where conflict never happens. It is one where conflict does not destroy the dignity and safety of either person.

## Physical Intimacy Is Not the Foundation

This is also where my understanding of intimacy connects with my understanding of relationships.

Physical intimacy can be an important part of a couple's relationship, but I do not think it should become the foundation of the relationship.

Imagine that two people have physical intimacy but do not understand each other, do not communicate openly, are not loyal, and do not respect each other.

That relationship is not healthy simply because physical intimacy exists.

On the other hand, two people can build emotional closeness through communication, shared experiences, cooking together, helping each other, spending time together, hugging, laughing, playing games, or simply sitting together in silence.

Physical intimacy can grow naturally from that connection.

## Giving Each Other Time

If my partner needs time to become comfortable with me, I would rather give her that time than treat intimacy as an obligation.

Maybe she needs a few months.

Maybe longer.

The important part is that both people should feel genuinely comfortable rather than one person feeling pressured by a socially defined expectation about what should happen after marriage.

I would want to build the relationship first.

I might cook for her, help her with daily responsibilities, spend time talking to her, learn about her habits, understand what makes her comfortable or uncomfortable, and allow her to understand me as well.

Eventually, if she genuinely feels comfortable and wants physical intimacy, that intimacy would have a completely different meaning because it would come from an established relationship rather than pressure or expectation.

That difference matters.

If physical intimacy had happened immediately when she was nervous, unfamiliar with me, and emotionally unprepared, there is a strong possibility that the experience would have felt very different from intimacy that develops after months of trust, comfort, communication, and emotional connection.

The point is not that intimacy after marriage should always be delayed.

The point is that readiness should belong to the people involved, not to a calendar.

## A Healthy Relationship Is a Two-Way Learning System

One thing I would not want is a relationship where I am always the teacher and she is always the student.

I would learn from her too.

I would want to understand which experiences shaped her, what made her happy, what hurt her, what she fears, how she handles difficult situations, and how she wants to grow.

I would also want us to regularly reflect on our relationship.

For example, I might ask:

"Which of my habits have been difficult for you?"

"Where do you think I need to improve?"

"Is there something I am doing that makes you feel uncomfortable?"

"Is there something you wish I understood better?"

And she should be able to ask me the same questions.

The purpose would not be to find faults in each other.

It would be to continuously improve the relationship.

## What a Healthy Relationship Means to Me

For me, a healthy relationship is not a relationship without problems.

It is a relationship where both people can face problems without turning against each other.

The four pillars are:

**Mutual Understanding:** We try to understand each other's perspective.

**Transparency:** We communicate our real thoughts, feelings, boundaries, and concerns instead of hiding important things.

**Loyalty:** We protect the relationship even when nobody is watching.

**Respect:** We never use anger, power, vulnerability, or physical strength as an excuse to degrade or harm each other.

From these foundations, other qualities can grow: love, trust, care, emotional safety, support, companionship, and intimacy.

And perhaps the most important part is that neither person should be finished learning.

A healthy relationship is a continuous process.

Two people meet with different experiences, different habits, different weaknesses, and different ways of thinking. They slowly learn each other, make mistakes, communicate, adjust, and grow.

So for me, a healthy relationship is not about finding a perfect person.

It is about two imperfect people creating an environment where both of them can continuously become better without losing themselves in the process.
