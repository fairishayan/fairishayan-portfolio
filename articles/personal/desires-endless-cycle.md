# Desires: The Endless Cycle of Life

Before exploring the depth of this concept, I want to begin with a few questions because they explain how I arrived at it:

- How did this concept first develop in my mind?
- What kind of imagination did I use to understand it through real-world situations?
- What methods and approaches do humans use to fulfil their desires?
- What resources did I use to collect information and develop this idea?

So, let's begin.

## The Beginning of the Concept

Desires play an important role in human life. They give us something to pursue. When we want something badly enough, we become willing to put effort into achieving it.

But I started wondering whether desire is actually the most efficient way to achieve our goals.

It isn't necessarily.

Discipline can often accomplish the same thing more efficiently than desire. The difference is that desire gives us an emotional reason to act, while discipline allows us to act even when that emotional motivation disappears.

That led me to a question:

**Why do most people focus more on desire than discipline?**

From my perspective, part of the answer lies in how we are raised.

From childhood, we learn what it feels like to receive something we want.

A toy.

A game.

A smartphone.

A chocolate.

A better bicycle.

Something we desperately asked our parents for.

And very often, those desires were connected to a task.

"Eat properly for three days and you will get the toy."

"Score 80% in school and you will get a new geometry box."

"Do well in your tests and you will get chocolate."

"Pass Class 10 with good marks and you will get a smartphone."

Looking at this psychologically, childhood can sometimes resemble a very simple reinforcement-learning environment.

We perform an action.

We receive a reward.

We learn that the action was worth performing.

Then the difficulty of the next task increases.

If we succeed, expectations often increase.

If we fail, expectations may temporarily decrease.

Over time, we learn an important association:

**Effort → Achievement → Reward → Satisfaction → New Desire**

And eventually, this becomes so normal that we stop questioning it.

## How Desires Become a Part of Life

As we grow older, the objects change, but the underlying pattern often remains.

As children, we want toys.

Then gadgets.

Then smartphones.

Then freedom.

Then academic success.

Then a good college.

Then a good career.

Then a better job.

Then a higher salary.

Then a better position.

Then marriage.

Then a life partner.

Then children.

Then good schools for those children.

Then good grades.

Then a successful career for them.

And eventually, we may begin deciding what our children should become.

Doctor.

Scientist.

Engineer.

IAS officer.

IPS officer.

Business owner.

The cycle continues.

The strange part is that we often don't realize that we are inside a cycle at all.

We simply move from one desire to another.

College is a good example.

Why do we go to college every day?

For attendance?

For marks?

For a degree?

For employment?

For social recognition?

Whatever the answer is, once one objective is completed, another appears.

The semester ends.

A new semester begins.

The degree ends.

The job search begins.

The job begins.

Then we want an increment.

Then a better designation.

Then a better organization.

Then financial security.

And the process continues.

**Desires rarely disappear. They usually change their subject.**

## The Difference Between Desire and Discipline

There is another problem.

Many people reach adulthood without ever being properly taught what discipline actually means.

In many educational environments, discipline is reduced to things such as:

- Don't make noise in class.
- Come to school on time.
- Keep your nails trimmed.
- Don't grow your hair too long.
- Complete your homework.
- Behave properly around teachers.

These things are not useless, but they represent only a very small part of discipline.

Real discipline is much broader.

It is the ability to continue doing what is necessary even when you no longer feel motivated to do it.

That distinction matters because desire is emotionally rewarding.

Discipline often isn't.

If I want a new laptop, I feel excited about getting it.

If I want good grades, imagining the result can motivate me.

But studying every day when I don't feel like studying is not nearly as emotionally exciting.

So humans naturally gravitate toward desire.

We already understand how desire feels.

We don't necessarily understand how to live without constantly needing a new desire to push us forward.

## How the Idea Entered My Mind

The honest answer is probably overthinking.

Or, as I sometimes describe it, maladaptive daydreaming.

I was basically giving imaginary lectures and having imaginary expert discussions inside my own head. Somehow, one of those imaginary discussions produced the idea of the Endless Cycle.

But the concept became much deeper during a period when I was extremely ill.

One night, while trying to sleep, I had a very strong feeling that I might not wake up the next morning.

I wasn't deliberately forcing myself to think about death. It was simply the feeling I was experiencing.

Then I imagined a situation:

What if God appeared in front of me and said:

**"You are going to die tonight. You have one final wish. What do you want?"**

I imagined myself thinking about the question.

And I couldn't find an answer.

I had more than thirty minutes to think about it, and still, I couldn't identify a final desire that felt important enough.

I could have asked for money.

I could have asked for experiences I had never had.

I could have asked for extraordinary abilities.

I could have asked for almost anything.

But then I realized something:

**I didn't actually need any of it.**

That realization surprised me.

Throughout my life, I had wanted many unnecessary things.

As a child, I wanted toys.

Then PC games.

Then a smartphone.

Then internet access.

Later, I wanted freedom to travel.

There were times when I was not allowed to travel independently, and eventually I decided that I would find a way to become independent anyway.

I travelled thousands of kilometres over the following years.

I wanted new phones.

I got them.

At one point, one of those phones was stolen, and another one was eventually purchased.

Looking back, I realized something complicated about my relationship with my desires.

I had received a lot.

I had also fought for a lot.

I had been supported.

I had also been criticized.

Both sides of that experience shaped me.

Yet when I imagined myself facing death, none of those desires seemed important anymore.

I didn't regret not having more money.

I didn't regret not owning more things.

I didn't regret not experiencing everything I could have experienced.

What I wanted at that moment was surprisingly simple:

**Peace.**

No chaos.

No violence.

No unnecessary drama.

Just peace, silence, and the freedom to leave without unfinished emotional noise.

That was when the concept stopped being merely something I had imagined.

I realized I had actually experienced it.

## The Formula I Eventually Reached

From that experience, I developed three ideas:

**Less Desire + Some Unpredictability = More Happiness**

**More Desire + Predictability = Less Happiness + More Chaos**

**Minimal Desire + Freedom from Constant Expectations = Greater Peace**

I don't consider these mathematical formulas.

They are simply the way I currently understand my own experience.

The fewer things I believe I absolutely need in order to be happy, the less control those things have over me.

And the more predictable my happiness becomes dependent on external outcomes, the easier it becomes for life to disappoint me.

## The Imagination Experiment

To explore this idea further, I started creating hypothetical situations in my imagination.

I would imagine someone telling me:

**"Everything you want will be given to you."**

My response was:

**"That doesn't mean my desires will end."**

If one desire is fulfilled, another usually appears.

The object changes.

The intensity changes.

The circumstances change.

But the mechanism remains.

This became particularly interesting when I applied it to relationships.

Imagine someone enters a relationship where every reasonable desire is immediately fulfilled.

At first, the desire might simply be to spend more time together.

Then that becomes normal.

Then the person wants more affection.

Then greater physical intimacy.

Then progressively more intense experiences.

The exact sequence will differ from person to person, but the underlying psychological pattern can remain the same:

**What was once extraordinary eventually becomes normal.**

And once something becomes normal, it stops providing the same psychological reward.

The mind starts looking for something new.

That is the part of the example that interests me.

The issue isn't the specific activity.

The issue is **adaptation**.

A desire is fulfilled.

Satisfaction appears.

The experience becomes familiar.

The excitement decreases.

A new desire emerges.

The cycle begins again.

## Best Case, Average Case, and Worst Case

I also started looking at how people behave when their desires are not automatically fulfilled.

### The Best Case

In the best-case scenario, everything a person asks for is willingly accepted by the other person.

The relationship can therefore progress according to mutual desire and consent.

But even here, the cycle does not necessarily stop.

Once something becomes normal, the mind can begin searching for something new.

So even unlimited access to desired experiences does not necessarily produce unlimited happiness.

### The Average Case

In an average situation, one person's desires will sometimes conflict with another person's boundaries.

This is where psychology can become complicated.

People may try persuasion, emotional pressure, insecurity, jealousy, withdrawal, or other forms of influence to get what they want.

The important observation for my concept is not that these techniques are effective.

It is that **desire can make people increasingly creative in finding ways to obtain what they want.**

The stronger the desire becomes, the more likely a person may be to rationalize behavior that they would otherwise consider questionable.

That applies far beyond relationships.

People manipulate situations to get jobs.

To get money.

To obtain approval.

To gain social status.

To convince their parents.

To influence friends.

Sometimes they don't even consciously recognize what they are doing.

### The Worst Case

The most interesting case to me is when both people are highly self-aware.

Suppose one person has strong boundaries and knows exactly what they are comfortable with.

The other person then has two choices.

They can respect those boundaries.

Or they can attempt to psychologically influence the person until those boundaries change.

That distinction reveals something important about desire.

Psychological intelligence can be used in two completely different ways.

It can help us understand someone better.

Or it can help us control them.

Those are not the same thing.

Understanding someone is not automatically manipulation.

Manipulation begins when understanding another person's psychology becomes a tool for overriding their autonomy.

This is where I draw a line for myself.

If I understand someone's fears, insecurities, decision-making patterns, emotional triggers, or vulnerabilities, that knowledge should not automatically become a weapon.

Otherwise, intelligence simply becomes another mechanism for fulfilling desire.

And that would make the cycle even worse.

## Desire Exists Outside Relationships

The same pattern appears in completely ordinary situations.

For example, I already had a good laptop, but I wanted a more powerful one.

I didn't initially have a strong enough practical reason to justify the upgrade.

So I changed my own direction.

I became more focused on AI research rather than simply AI engineering.

Now the requirements became different.

Research simulations.

Larger models.

More demanding workloads.

Model training.

More computational resources.

Suddenly, the desire had a justification.

This showed me something interesting about myself:

Sometimes humans don't simply search for a way to achieve their goals.

We also change the explanation surrounding the goal.

The desire comes first.

The reasoning comes afterward.

## Using Psychology to Influence Outcomes

I have also noticed this pattern in academic situations.

For example, I sometimes prepare notes in advance and show them to teachers, asking whether there are any mistakes or flaws.

On the surface, I am asking for feedback.

But psychologically, there is another effect.

It reminds the teacher that I am someone who studies independently and prepares in advance.

When exams arrive, that existing perception can influence how my work is evaluated.

Again, the interesting part for me isn't simply whether the technique works.

It is that humans constantly influence each other's perceptions, often without explicitly acknowledging it.

I have also experimented with psychological influence among friends, partly to understand my own abilities.

But I learned something more important from that:

**Manipulation requires trust.**

If someone doesn't trust you, influencing them becomes much harder.

Trust creates psychological safety.

Psychological safety creates access.

And access creates influence.

That is why trust is so powerful.

It can become the foundation of genuine intimacy, or it can become the foundation of manipulation.

The difference is what someone chooses to do with that trust.

## The Resources Behind the Concept

I did not develop this idea from one academic paper or one particular book.

A lot of it came from observing different kinds of stories and fictional situations.

I watched films and dramas, including stories involving relationships, heartbreak, wealth, and extreme lifestyles.

Sometimes the movies themselves were honestly terrible.

But even bad stories can provide interesting observations if you stop watching them only for entertainment.

I started asking:

Why do wealthy characters sometimes develop very different desires?

Why does extreme freedom sometimes produce unusual behavior?

Why do some people become deeply unhappy despite having everything?

Why does heartbreak affect some people so severely?

Why do some people constantly search for new experiences?

Then I began creating hypothetical scenarios around those questions.

One pattern repeatedly appeared.

Extreme behavior usually doesn't begin at an extreme level.

It starts normally.

A small desire is fulfilled.

Then another.

Then another.

Eventually, the person reaches a point that would have seemed unimaginable at the beginning.

## Wealth, Freedom, and the Value of Small Things

This also made me think about the difference between a privileged life and an ordinary one.

Someone who has almost unlimited access to money, entertainment, travel, relationships, and experiences may have the freedom to explore desires much further and much earlier in life.

Someone struggling financially, physically, or mentally may have completely different priorities.

Their biggest desire might simply be stability.

A day without financial stress.

A good meal.

A little free time.

A small gift.

A peaceful evening.

And this creates an interesting paradox.

The same event can produce completely different levels of happiness depending on how accessible that event normally is.

A small unexpected gift may make an ordinary person extremely happy.

The same gift might mean almost nothing to someone who can buy whatever they want whenever they want.

So perhaps happiness is not determined only by what we receive.

It is also influenced by how much we have already become accustomed to receiving.

## The Endless Cycle

This is ultimately what I mean by **"Desires: The Endless Cycle of Life."**

Desire begins with something simple.

We want it.

We work for it.

We obtain it.

We feel satisfied.

Then the satisfaction fades.

The mind adapts.

A new desire appears.

And the process begins again.

The object can change from toys to smartphones, from smartphones to education, from education to careers, from careers to relationships, from relationships to family, and eventually from our own lives to the lives of our children.

Then we may unknowingly pass the same system to the next generation.

We tell our children what they should want.

They achieve it.

Then we give them another target.

Eventually, they become adults who know exactly how to pursue desires but may never have been taught how to question them.

And perhaps that is the deepest part of the concept.

**The problem isn't that humans have desires.**

Desire is necessary.

It gives us ambition.

It gives us direction.

It can push us toward growth.

The problem begins when we stop asking:

**"Why do I want this?"**

**"Will getting this actually make my life better?"**

**"What will I want after I get it?"**

**"Am I pursuing this because I genuinely want it, or because I was taught that I should want it?"**

And perhaps the most important question is:

**"If I received everything I currently desire, would I finally be satisfied?"**

If the answer is no, then the problem may not be that we haven't achieved enough.

Maybe we simply haven't understood the cycle yet.

That is what I currently believe about desires.

They don't necessarily disappear when we fulfil them.

They evolve.

They move from one target to another.

And unless we become conscious of that process, we can spend an entire lifetime moving from one fulfilled desire to the next without ever stopping to ask whether we were actually happy along the way.

**Desires may be endless.

But our lives are not.**
