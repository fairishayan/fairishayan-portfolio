# What Kind of Father I Want to Become and What Kind of Life I Want to Give My Children

I do not want to become a perfect father.

I simply want to become a good one.

More specifically, I do not want to repeat the parts of my own father's parenting that I believe hurt me. My father has given me many things that I am grateful for. He has supported my education and helped me reach my bachelor's degree, and he may continue supporting my master's education as well.

So this is not an argument that my father was a bad father.

He wasn't.

But there are things I have expected from him as a son that I never felt he really understood. Some of those expectations are actually very simple: I wanted my father to spend time with his children, understand them, listen to their problems, and try to understand their feelings.

I have had many problems in my life that I could never comfortably discuss with him.

The reason is not that I believe he would simply disagree with me. I expect that if I told him about some of my problems, he might blame me for creating them, criticize my decisions, or dismiss what I am feeling as unnecessary drama.

Eventually, you stop sharing things with someone when you already know how the conversation will end.

That is something I do not want my children to experience with me.

## Why I Want to Be Different

There is an important question here:

Am I simply trying to become the opposite of my father?

To some extent, yes.

But I do not think that is the whole story.

I have observed my father for years. I have seen how he behaves when I am going through difficult situations, how he can be physically present but emotionally absent, and how strongly he focuses on expectations and rules.

At some point, I started asking myself why his words affect me so much less than they used to.

Why can I become angry with him?

Why do I sometimes stop caring about what he expects from me?

I think part of the answer is that a relationship cannot survive only on authority.

If one person constantly tries to control another person while ignoring what the other person needs, eventually the controlled person may stop emotionally responding to that authority.

That experience taught me something important.

I do not want my children to obey me simply because I am their father.

I want them to respect me because they understand me, trust me, and know that I genuinely care about them.

So I am not trying to become my father's opposite just for the sake of being different.

I want to take what was good in his parenting, understand what did not work for me, and improve upon it.

In that sense, even some of my father's weaknesses have taught me something valuable.

## My Family Would Be My Priority

If I become a father, I want my family to be one of the highest priorities in my life.

I want to work hard, build a career, become financially independent, and create a stable life.

But I do not want professional success to become an excuse for emotional absence.

I have seen how easily work can consume someone's entire day. A person can spend years saying, "I'm doing this for my family," while barely spending meaningful time with them.

I do not want to give my children everything financially while giving them almost nothing emotionally.

I want to be present.

Not just physically present.

Mentally present.

## I Want to Learn With My Children

I do not want to be the father who tells his children:

"Wake up early."

"Go for a walk."

"Exercise."

"Study."

while I remain somewhere else doing something completely different.

If I want my children to exercise, I want to wake up with them.

We could go running together.

We could play football.

We could exercise together.

Sometimes I would let them win.

Sometimes I would win.

I do not want them to grow up believing that they are always losing, but I also do not want them to believe that life will always let them win.

I want them to experience both.

The same principle applies to learning.

I would want to teach them at home, help them with their homework, solve puzzles with them, discuss mathematics, and introduce them to logical reasoning.

But I would also be willing to learn things myself before teaching them.

If my child asks me something I do not know, I do not want to pretend that I know.

I want to be able to say:

"I don't know. Let's find out."

That might be one of the most important things a child can learn from a parent.

## I Want Curiosity Before Competition

I would not want education to begin and end with school marks.

I want my children to encounter ideas from a very young age.

Cosmology could become a game:

A for Andromeda.

B for Black Hole.

C for Cluster.

D for Dark Matter.

Computer science could become:

A for Algorithms.

B for Brute Force.

C for Cryptography.

D for Data Mining.

Biology could begin with:

A for Amoeba.

B for Bacteria.

The purpose would not be to turn a five-year-old into a scientist.

It would simply be to make the world interesting.

I want them to grow up thinking:

"There is so much I don't know."

rather than:

"I already know enough."

## I Do Not Want to Force Their Career

I would never want to decide what my children must become.

If one child loves engineering, I will support engineering.

If another loves biology, I will support biology.

If another wants to become an artist, musician, writer, teacher, researcher, entrepreneur, or something I cannot even imagine today, I want to understand why.

My job as a parent would be to expose them to possibilities, not select their destination.

I can show them different roads.

They should eventually decide which road they want to walk.

This is also where I recognize a contradiction in my own thinking.

I have talked about teaching my children psychology, human behavior, manipulation, and other concepts from a young age.

But mastery cannot be forced.

If they have no interest in something, I cannot manufacture genuine curiosity simply by teaching harder.

So the better approach would be exposure.

I can introduce ideas.

If they become interested, I can go deeper with them.

If they don't, I should let them move toward something that actually interests them.

## Teaching Them to Recognize Manipulation

There is another important distinction here.

I do not want my children to become manipulators.

I want them to understand manipulation well enough to recognize it.

I would like to teach them how human behavior can reveal information.

For example, if someone suddenly changes the subject whenever a particular question is asked, that can be worth noticing.

If someone repeatedly distracts them whenever they try to discuss something important, that pattern can be worth questioning.

If someone uses their emotional vulnerabilities against them, I want them to recognize what is happening.

I could teach them these things through real situations, stories, observation, and conversations.

But the goal should be protection, not exploitation.

Knowing how manipulation works and using that knowledge to manipulate people are completely different things.

I want my children to understand the first without becoming dependent on the second.

## I Cannot Protect Them From Every Failure

This is another part I have had to think about.

I want my children to be strong.

I do not want someone to easily exploit them, manipulate them, break their trust, or destroy their confidence.

But can I realistically prevent them from ever experiencing those things?

No.

They will fail.

They will make terrible decisions.

Someone may lie to them.

Someone may betray them.

They may trust the wrong person.

They may fall in love with someone who hurts them.

They may fail an examination.

They may lose an opportunity.

They may misunderstand someone.

And sometimes they may be completely responsible for their own mistake.

My role should not be to eliminate every possibility of pain.

My role should be to make sure they know they can come back to me after experiencing it.

I want to become their courage bank.

When they fail, I want to stand beside them.

Not to erase the consequences.

Not to tell them that they were automatically right.

But to help them stand up again.

## Vulnerability Is Not Weakness

I have also realized something about the way I think about strength.

I sometimes think that if a person understands psychology well enough, they can protect themselves from almost anything.

But that can become unhealthy if taken too far.

A person does not have to become emotionally untouchable.

Vulnerability is not weakness.

My children should be allowed to be scared.

They should be allowed to cry.

They should be allowed to say:

"I don't know what to do."

They should be allowed to trust someone.

They should be allowed to make mistakes.

I want to teach them how to protect their vulnerabilities, not how to be ashamed of having them.

Even I have vulnerabilities.

Being strong does not mean having none.

It means understanding them well enough to protect yourself while still remaining human.

## I Want to Spend Time With Them

I want to travel with my children.

I want to show them different cities, cultures, environments, and countries.

My father has travelled to places such as Kashmir, Mumbai, and Bhopal, but because of his responsibilities, we did not always get the opportunity to travel with him.

I want to experience those things with my own children.

I want them to see that the world is much larger than the environment in which they were born.

I want them to meet different kinds of people and understand that different cultures can have completely different ways of living.

Travel, for me, would not just be tourism.

It would be education.

## About Screen Time

I would ideally keep screen exposure extremely limited during their earliest years, and I would also have to follow the same rule myself.

There is little value in telling children to stop using screens while the parent spends the entire day staring at one.

If I want them to explore the physical world, I should explore it with them.

If I want them to read, I should read.

If I want them to exercise, I should exercise.

Children learn much more from what parents do than from what parents say.

## I Want to Feed Them Myself

There is something that might look strange to other people, but I genuinely value it.

I want to feed my children with my own hands even when they are older, as long as they are comfortable with it.

I have seen families where parents continue these small acts of care even when their children become adults.

To me, these things are not necessarily about dependence.

Sometimes they are simply expressions of affection.

At the same time, I recognize that independence matters too.

So I do not want "care" to become an excuse for preventing my children from learning how to take care of themselves.

The goal would be affection without dependency.

## Freedom and Independence

I want my children to have freedom.

When they reach adulthood, I want them to make increasingly important decisions for themselves.

By the time they reach university, I would want them to have significant control over where and what they study.

If they want to study in another city or another country, I would try to support that if it is realistically possible.

In fact, I think sending them away for university could be an important part of becoming independent.

There comes a point where a parent's constant presence can become a disadvantage.

They need to learn how to cook, manage money, deal with difficult people, solve problems, make decisions, fail, recover, and live without immediately asking their parents what to do.

I want to be available when they genuinely need me.

But I do not want them to need me for everything.

## I Also Need a Life Outside Parenting

There is another thing I almost ignored when I first imagined my ideal family.

My children are not going to be my entire identity.

My wife and I will also need our own relationship.

We will need private time, personal space, conversations that have nothing to do with parenting, and the ability to maintain our relationship as two people rather than existing only as "Mom and Dad."

My children should also have their own personal space.

A healthy family does not mean everyone is together every minute.

Closeness and independence can exist together.

I want my children to know:

"My parents love me."

and also:

"My parents trust me enough to let me live my own life."

That balance is important.

## I Want to Ask My Children for Feedback

One unusual thing I would like to do is ask my children what they think about my parenting.

Not because children should become the parents.

But because parents can also make mistakes.

I would want to ask them:

"What do you think I am doing well?"

"What do you think I am doing badly?"

"Is there something you wish I understood?"

"Do you feel I give you enough freedom?"

"Do you feel I listen to you?"

I want them to be able to criticize me respectfully.

If I expect them to learn from their mistakes, I should also be willing to learn from mine.

## What I Absolutely Do Not Want to Do

I do not want to hit my children.

I do not want to humiliate them.

I do not want to use insults as discipline.

I do not want to constantly compare them with other children.

I do not want to make my love conditional on their academic performance.

I do not want to decide their career for them.

I do not want to force them into relationships or marriage.

I do not want to use fear to make them obedient.

And I do not want them to believe that being my child means they owe me their entire life.

I brought them into the world.

That does not give me ownership over them.

## What I Actually Want From Them

I do not want perfect children.

I want curious children.

Children who can think.

Children who can question me.

Children who can disagree with me respectfully.

Children who can recognize manipulation.

Children who can protect themselves.

Children who can admit when they are wrong.

Children who can love without losing themselves.

Children who can be vulnerable without feeling ashamed.

Children who can eventually leave home and build a life of their own without feeling guilty for becoming independent.

And most importantly, I want them to know that if life becomes unbearable, they can still come home.

## The Biggest Question I Have About My Own Thinking

There are several contradictions in my own idea of fatherhood.

The first is whether I am simply trying to become the opposite of my father.

I think there is some truth in that.

But I also believe I am taking the lessons I learned from watching him and trying to refine them.

The second is whether my desire to be extremely involved in my children's lives could become over-parenting.

That risk is real.

Even good intentions can become excessive.

If I guide them from breakfast until bedtime, make every decision with them, solve every problem for them, and remain constantly present, I might accidentally make independence harder.

So I need to remember that being present does not mean being everywhere.

Sometimes the best thing a parent can do is step back.

The third contradiction is freedom versus training.

I say I will not force my children to study, but I also imagine teaching them psychology, science, technology, critical thinking, and human behavior from an early age.

The answer, I think, is that exposure is different from force.

I can introduce them to knowledge.

I can make learning interesting.

I can answer their questions.

But if they develop a different interest, I have to respect it.

Mastery cannot be forced.

The fourth contradiction is strength versus vulnerability.

I want my children to be difficult to manipulate, but I do not want them to become emotionally closed.

I want them to know how to protect themselves without believing that needing someone makes them weak.

And finally, there is the possibility that my own pain is influencing the kind of father I imagine becoming.

My experiences have shaped my definition of good parenting.

That is not necessarily a bad thing.

But I need to continuously monitor myself so that my children do not become a project through which I try to repair my own childhood.

They deserve to be their own people.

## The Father I Ultimately Want to Become

I do not want to be the father who says:

"I know what is best for you because I am your father."

I want to be the father who says:

"I have more life experience than you, so let me help you understand the options. But eventually, this is your life."

I want to be present without being controlling.

Protective without being restrictive.

Involved without becoming dependent on my children's dependence.

Disciplined without being authoritarian.

Strong without being emotionally unavailable.

And loving without making love conditional.

My children will eventually grow up.

They will leave the house.

They will build relationships I cannot control.

They will make decisions I may disagree with.

They may choose careers I never expected.

They may live hundreds or thousands of kilometres away.

They may even build a life that looks completely different from the one I imagined for them.

And if I have done my job properly, I should be able to let them go.

Because the goal of parenting is not to create children who cannot live without their parents.

The goal is to raise people who can live independently, think for themselves, protect themselves, love others, handle failure, and still know that they have a home where they are accepted.

That is the kind of father I want to become.
